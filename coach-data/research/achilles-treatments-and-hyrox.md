# Achilles treatment options and the Hyrox question

Written 2026-10-03, after the orthopedist's diagnosis on 10/2: likely micro-tearing and
inflammation where the left Achilles attaches to the heel, with spur risk if it stays
irritated. X-rays clean. No MRI unless it keeps giving way or gets worse. Treatment
choice: home rehab, no formal PT.

---

## Part 1 — Treatments the orthopedist mentioned

### Bottom line

| Option | Evidence for this injury | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive loading (the home program) | Strongest; the main treatment in every guideline | Backbone. Do this regardless. |
| Shockwave | Good for the insertional type specifically | Reasonable add-on if stalled after 6–8 weeks |
| Therapeutic ultrasound | Weak | Skip unless it's free |
| PRP (plasma) | Large, well-run trials show no benefit over a sham | Not worth it now |
| Stem cells | Small, low-quality studies | Not recommended |
| Peptides (BPC-157, TB-500) | No human trials for tendon healing | Not recommended |
| Oral collagen + vitamin C | Small but positive Achilles trial | Cheap, low-risk add-on |
| Steroid injection | Short relief, rupture risk | Avoid near the Achilles |

### Steroid (cortisone) injection

Not on the orthopedist's list, but some clinics offer it, so it's worth knowing. Steroids
calm pain for a few weeks but weaken tendon tissue, and injections in or around the
Achilles have been linked to ruptures. Taking away the pain also takes away the warning
signal while the tendon is weaker. **Avoid.**

### PRP (platelet-rich plasma)

Your own blood is spun down to concentrate platelets, which carry growth factors, and
injected into the tendon. The idea is to kick-start healing.

- **The best trial:** Kearney et al., *JAMA* 2021. 240 people with chronic Achilles
  tendinopathy across 24 sites got either one PRP injection or a sham (a needle that
  didn't enter the tendon). At 6 months, function scores were essentially identical
  (54.4 vs 53.4), and every secondary outcome was also the same.
- **An earlier trial** (de Vos et al., *JAMA* 2010) found the same thing: PRP plus
  rehab was no better than saline plus rehab.
- **Caveat:** both trials studied the mid-tendon type. Your injury is at the insertion,
  and there's little good research on PRP there. No evidence suggests it would work
  better at the insertion.
- **Practical cost:** usually out of pocket, often several hundred to a couple of
  thousand dollars, and it typically means 1–2 weeks of reduced loading afterward,
  which pauses the rehab that actually works.

**Verdict:** not worth it now. At most, a last resort after many months of failed
loading plus shockwave, and even then the odds are poor.

### Stem cells and other "orthobiologics"

Cells from your bone marrow or fat, or donor products, injected into the tendon.

- A systematic review of stem cell therapy for tendon problems found 8 trials
  (289 patients), all with moderate to high risk of bias. One Achilles trial reported
  faster recovery with donor-derived cells than with PRP. Others used stem cells
  alongside surgery, which doesn't apply to you.
- Clinic products vary widely. Some marketed as "stem cell" treatments (for example,
  some amniotic or umbilical products) may contain few or no live stem cells. These
  products aren't FDA-approved for orthopedic use.
- Cost often runs into the thousands, out of pocket.

**Verdict:** interesting research area, not a treatment with proof behind it.
Not recommended.

### Peptides (BPC-157, TB-500) — detailed pros and cons (expanded 10/3)

Lab-made short proteins, usually self-injected under the skin near the injury.
**BPC-157** is a 15-amino-acid fragment of a protein found in stomach juice.
**TB-500** is a synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4, a protein involved in cell
movement and wound repair.

**Possible upsides**
- **Animal and lab data are genuinely encouraging.** In rats, BPC-157 has sped healing
  of cut Achilles tendons and tendon-to-bone attachments, and in cell studies it
  increases tendon-cell growth and blood-vessel formation. Thymosin beta-4 has similar
  wound-repair data.
- **No serious harms reported so far** in the limited human use that's been studied.
- **Access may become more legitimate.** After the July 2026 advisory vote, if the FDA
  finalizes it, licensed compounding pharmacies could make BPC-157 with real quality
  control, which would beat today's online vials.

**Downsides**
- **Almost no human evidence.** The total published human data for BPC-157 is a few
  small pilot studies and conference abstracts. The best-known is a 2021 knee study:
  17 patients, no control group, no placebo, and patient-reported improvement, so you
  can't separate the drug from natural healing or placebo. Nothing in humans tests
  Achilles or tendon healing.
- **Unknown long-term safety.** No study has followed people for long. Both peptides
  work partly by encouraging new blood vessels and cell growth. That's how they might
  help a tendon, and it's also why researchers raise a theoretical concern about
  feeding tumors. It's unproven either way, but nobody has checked.
- **Quality is a gamble today.** Most supply is sold "for research use only," with
  independent tests often finding wrong doses or contamination. Injecting anything
  also carries infection risk.
- **Regulatory status is unsettled.** Flagged by the FDA in 2023 (immune reactions,
  no human data), removed from that list in April 2026, recommended 8–6 by an advisory
  committee in July 2026 against FDA staff's advice, and not yet finalized as far as
  I could find. A recommendation that pharmacies may compound it says nothing about
  whether it works.
- **Banned in competition** by the World Anti-Doping Agency (BPC-157 since 2022;
  TB-500 explicitly). Only matters for drug-tested events.
- **The hidden cost: false confidence.** Pain relief isn't tendon strength. If a
  peptide makes the heel feel better, the temptation is to run sooner than the tendon
  can handle. An insertional Achilles only remodels in response to progressive load,
  and no injection replaces that.

**If you try one anyway**
1. Through a licensed physician, using a state-licensed compounding pharmacy, not
   an online "research" seller.
2. Don't change the return-to-run criteria. The peptide doesn't earn you a shortcut.
3. Start it in a week with nothing else new (no shockwave, no big program jump) and
   log the date, so the Sunday numbers can show whether anything changed.
4. Stop and see a doctor for injection-site redness, swelling, fever, or a
   giving-way moment.

**The peptide with actual human Achilles evidence: oral collagen**
- **Praet et al., 2019:** 20 people with Achilles tendinopathy did a twice-daily calf
  program for 6 months, with collagen peptides for 3 months and a placebo for 3
  (crossover). Function scores rose about 12.6 points in 3 months on collagen versus
  about 5.3 on placebo; 12 of 18 finishers were back to running at 3 months. It's small
  and used the manufacturer's product, so treat it as promising, not proven.
- **Shaw et al., 2017:** 15 g of gelatin with vitamin C taken an hour before a short
  bout of jumping roughly doubled a blood marker of collagen production.
- **How to use it:** 10–15 g of collagen peptides or gelatin plus about 50 mg of
  vitamin C, 30–60 minutes before the rehab session. Cheap and low risk. Collagen is
  usually bovine or marine, so it fits gluten-free, dairy-free, and soy-free; check
  the label for additives.

**Verdict:** injectable peptides are a speculative bet with real unknowns, not a
treatment. If you want a peptide, oral collagen before rehab is the one with human
Achilles data behind it.

### Shockwave (not an injection)

Sound-pressure pulses applied through the skin over the sore area, usually 3–5 weekly
sessions.

- **The key trial:** Rompe et al., 2008. 50 people with chronic insertional Achilles
  tendinopathy got either a loading program or low-energy shockwave. Shockwave did
  significantly better at 4 months on every measure.
- **Caveat that matters for you:** the loading program in that trial used heel drops
  off a step, which bends the ankle deeply and is known to irritate the insertion. Your
  program loads on flat ground for exactly that reason, so the comparison flatters
  shockwave a little. It's still the best-supported add-on for this type.
- Shockwave is also used for calcium build-up at the insertion, which matters given
  the spur warning.

**Verdict:** the one extra worth paying for, if you've stalled after 6–8 weeks of
consistent loading.

## Part 2 — HYROX as the next event

**Format:** 8 × 1 km runs, each followed by a station: SkiErg 1,000 m, sled push 50 m,
sled pull 50 m, burpee broad jumps 80 m, row 1,000 m, farmers carry 200 m, sandbag
lunges 100 m, wall balls 100 reps. Open men 2025/26 standards: sled push 152 kg,
sled pull 103 kg, farmers carry 2 × 24 kg, sandbag 20 kg, wall ball 6 kg to a 3 m
target.

### Why it fits you

- You're a former sprinter with strength you enjoy using. Hyrox rewards strength and
  power more than a half marathon does.
- Total running is 8 km in 1 km pieces, so training needs less weekly mileage than a
  half marathon block. That is easier on the tendons that keep flaring.
- It builds on the hip, adductor, and strength work we've already started.
- Strength-heavy training plus moderate running suits the weight-loss goal.

### Station-by-station load on the left Achilles insertion and post tib

| Station | Load on your injuries | Why | When it can return |
|---|---|---|---|
| SkiErg | Low | Upper body and hips; feet barely move | Now |
| Row | Low–moderate | The catch bends the ankle deeply; fix by lowering the foot stretcher and shortening the catch | Now, with that change |
| Sled pull | Moderate | Arms and back do most of the work, but heels dig in as you lean back | Achilles Phase 2, light |
| Farmers carry | Moderate | Walking under load is fine; jogging with it is harder on the tendon | Phase 2 |
| Wall balls | Moderate–high | 100 deep squats (deep ankle bend) plus rising onto the toes | Phase 2–3, partial depth first |
| Running (8 × 1 km) | Moderate–high | Impact, faster than easy pace, and indoor courses have lots of tight turns, the post tib's known trigger | Walk-run rebuild |
| Sandbag lunges | High | The back foot sits in deep ankle bend under load every step; also loads the adductors and knee | Phase 3 |
| Sled push | Very high | Driving off the forefoot with the ankle deeply bent against a 152 kg sled: exactly the bend-plus-pull the insertion can't tolerate yet | Last; after Phase 3 |
| Burpee broad jumps | Very high | Repeated jumping and landing, the Achilles' spring job at its hardest | Last; after the hop tests |

### What that means

- **Hyrox is not a way around the Achilles.** Three stations (sled push, burpee broad
  jumps, sandbag lunges) load the insertion harder than easy running does. It needs the
  full rehab, including the jumping phase, before race-specific training.
- **It does make good use of the recovery months.** SkiErg, the bike, modified rowing,
  upper body, and hip work are all Hyrox training and all safe now. The heavy calf work
  in Achilles Phase 2 is exactly what sled push and the jumps will need later.
- **The gentler entry is Doubles.** You run the full 8 km together, but split the
  station work with a partner, so your partner can take more of the burpee broad
  jumps, lunges, and sled push. The Relay (four people, each running 2 × 1 km and
  doing two stations) is the lightest option, for example choosing SkiErg and row.
- **Timeline:** about 6–12 weeks to get back to running, then 8–12 weeks of
  Hyrox-specific build including the jumping work. That points to an event around
  March–May 2027 at the earliest. Pick one after you've passed the return-to-run criteria.

## Part 3 — With hypothyroidism / Hashimoto's (added 10/3)

**The thyroid matters to this injury on its own.** Thyroid hormone is needed for
collagen production and for keeping tendon cells alive and working. Underactive thyroid
is linked to tendon problems, slower tendon healing, and calcium deposits in tendons.
That last one connects to the orthopedist's spur warning. Making sure your levels are
well controlled is a real healing lever, likely bigger than any injection. If labs
(TSH, free T4) haven't been checked in the last few months, ask your doctor to recheck.

**By treatment:**
- **Peptides (BPC-157, TB-500): the most concern.** Hashimoto's is an immune system
  attacking the thyroid. The FDA's stated worry about BPC-157 was immune reactions, and
  no study has tested these peptides in people with autoimmune disease, so the risk is
  unknown, not known to be zero. Gray-market vials can also carry bacterial
  contaminants that provoke immune reactions. Being autoimmune strengthens the case
  for skipping them. At minimum, clear it with whoever manages your thyroid.
- **Steroid injection:** still avoid for the tendon reasons. Steroids can also nudge
  thyroid lab results for a while, so tell your doctor if you ever get one before labs.
- **PRP:** your own blood, and Hashimoto's isn't a reason to avoid it. The problem is
  still that it didn't beat a sham in the best trial.
- **Stem cells:** your own cells raise no specific thyroid concern. Donor products
  add an unknown immune factor. Same verdict as before: not recommended.
- **Shockwave:** no thyroid concern.
- **Oral collagen:** fine, but timing matters if you take thyroid medication
  (levothyroxine or similar). Food and protein reduce its absorption, so take the
  medication on an empty stomach and the collagen at least 30–60 minutes later.
  Calcium and iron, which are common in multivitamins, interfere more, so keep the
  multivitamin about 4 hours from the medication. If the multivitamin contains biotin,
  skip it for 2–3 days before thyroid blood tests, because biotin can distort results.

**One more Achilles safety note, unrelated to the thyroid:** fluoroquinolone
antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) carry an FDA warning for tendon rupture,
especially at the Achilles. If one is ever prescribed, mention your Achilles and ask
whether there's an alternative.
